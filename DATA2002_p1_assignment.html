2.1 RQ1: Work hours and cramming

Research Question: Is there a difference in weekly paid employment hours between students who cram before assessments and those who do not?

Weekly paid employment hours are often considered a key factor affecting student engagement. This research question examines whether work hours are associated with students’ assessment strategy, specifically whether they tend to cram before deadlines. If time-pressured students are more likely to delay revision until the last minute, this insight could help the teaching team decide whether more flexible study support is needed for students with heavy work commitments. A Welch two-sample t-test is used to compare mean weekly work hours between Cram and Non-Cram students.

2.1.2 Hypotheses

Let \(\mu_1\) be the mean weekly work hours for Cram students and \(\mu_2\) for Non-Cram students.

\(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\)
\(H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2\)

2.1.3 Assumptions

Code 
manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)
  m <- mean(x)
  (sum((x - m)^3) / n) / (sum((x - m)^2) / n)^(3/2)
}

2.1.5 P-value

The corresponding p-value is \(p = 0.0004\). We calculate Cohen’s d to quantify the size of this effect.

Code 
pooled_sd <- sqrt(((length(cram_log) - 1) * var(cram_log) +
                    (length(noncram_log) - 1) * var(noncram_log)) /
                    (length(cram_log) + length(noncram_log) - 2))
cohend <- (mean(cram_log) - mean(noncram_log)) / pooled_sd
cohend
[1] 0.3721456

Cohen’s d = 0.372, indicating a small-to-medium effect size.

2.1.6 Conclusion&Limitations

Since \(p = 0.0004 < 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). Cram students report significantly higher weekly work hours (log scale) than Non-Cram students, with a small-to-medium effect (Cohen’s \(d = 0.372\)), suggesting that paid work is one of several factors associated with cramming. Although zero-inflation remained after log transformation, the large sample size supports the t-test approximation. As the data are observational, this association cannot establish that longer work hours cause cramming.

2.2.6 Conclusion

We reject \(H_0\): multitasking and cramming are associated, though \(V = 0.17\) makes it one correlate of cramming rather than a defining feature.

2.2.7 Limitations

Both variables are self-reported, so “cram” and “multi-task” may be applied inconsistently, and collapsing assess discards information. This observational, voluntary-response survey cannot establish causation — shared workload or procrastination could drive both — and may not generalise.

The two-sided permutation p-value is calculated by counting simulated statistics at least as extreme as the observed statistic in either direction:

extreme_count <- sum(abs(t_permuted) >= abs(t_observed))

p_permutation <- (extreme_count + 1) / (B + 1)

We formally test the difference using a two-sided permutation test with the Welch \(t\)-statistic as the resampling statistic.

  1. Hypotheses: Let \(\mu_Y\) and \(\mu_N\) be the population mean weekly paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask, respectively. \(H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0\) versus \(H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0\).

  2. Assumptions: Responses from different students are treated as independent. Under \(H_0\), multitasking labels are assumed exchangeable. A normal population is not required for the permutation p-value, although the mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.

  3. Test statistic: \(T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}\). The observed difference is 1.79 hours per week and \(t_0=2.172\).

  4. Permutation procedure: With set.seed(2002), the Yes/No labels were randomly reassigned \(B=10{,}000\) times while employment hours were held fixed. The Welch statistic was recalculated each time to form the null distribution in Figure 4.

  5. P-value: For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying \(|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|\) were counted. The plus-one estimate is \(p_{\mathrm{perm}}=0.0317\).

  6. Decision: Since \(p<0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). There is evidence that mean paid-employment hours differ by multitasking status; multitasking students worked 1.79 more hours per week on average in this sample. However, Cohen’s \(d=0.17\) indicates a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are self-reported, the 70-hour cleaning threshold is analyst-chosen, and confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey of DATA2x02 students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically generalise to other students.

## RQ1: Work hours and cramming

**Research Question:** Is there a difference in weekly paid employment hours between students who cram before assessments and those who do not?

Weekly paid employment hours are often considered a key factor affecting student engagement. This research question examines whether work hours are associated with students' assessment strategy, specifically whether they tend to cram before deadlines. If time-pressured students are more likely to delay revision until the last minute, this insight could help the teaching team decide whether more flexible study support is needed for students with heavy work commitments. A Welch two-sample t-test is used to compare mean weekly work hours between Cram and Non-Cram students.

### Hypotheses

Let $\mu_1$ be the mean weekly work hours for Cram students and $\mu_2$ for Non-Cram students.

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$  
$H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$

### Assumptions

The Welch two-sample t-test assumes approximate normality within each group. We test this on the raw data using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

```{r}
cram_raw <- rq1_data %>% filter(is_cram == "Cram") %>% pull(work_hrs)
noncram_raw <- rq1_data %>% filter(is_cram == "Non-Cram") %>% pull(work_hrs)
shapiro_cram_raw <- shapiro.test(cram_raw)
shapiro_noncram_raw <- shapiro.test(noncram_raw)
 
tibble::tibble(
  Group = c("Cram", "Non-Cram"),
  Statistic = c(shapiro_cram_raw$statistic, shapiro_noncram_raw$statistic),
  p_value = c(shapiro_cram_raw$p.value, shapiro_noncram_raw$p.value)
) %>%
  mutate(
    Statistic = round(Statistic, 3),
    p_value = format.pval(p_value, digits = 3, eps = 0.001)
  ) %>%
  knitr::kable(align = c("l", "c", "c"), caption = "Shapiro-Wilk test on raw work hours")
```

Both groups have Shapiro-Wilk p-values far below 0.05, so the raw data does not satisfy the normality assumption. We apply a log(x+1) transformation and re-check normality below.

```{r}
cram_log <- log1p(cram_raw)
noncram_log <- log1p(noncram_raw)
shapiro_cram_log <- shapiro.test(cram_log)
shapiro_noncram_log <- shapiro.test(noncram_log)
 
tibble::tibble(
  Group = c("Cram", "Non-Cram"),
  Statistic = c(shapiro_cram_log$statistic, shapiro_noncram_log$statistic),
  p_value = c(shapiro_cram_log$p.value, shapiro_noncram_log$p.value)
) %>%
  mutate(
    Statistic = round(Statistic, 3),
    p_value = format.pval(p_value, digits = 3, eps = 0.001)
  ) %>%
  knitr::kable(align = c("l", "c", "c"), caption = "Shapiro-Wilk test on log-transformed work hours")

manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)
  m <- mean(x)
  (sum((x - m)^3) / n) / (sum((x - m)^2) / n)^(3/2)
}
```

Normality is still not fully satisfied after transformation. We compare skewness before and after the transformation to assess whether the distribution shape has nonetheless improved.

```{r}
skew_cram_raw <- manual_skew(cram_raw)
skew_noncram_raw <- manual_skew(noncram_raw)
skew_cram_log <- manual_skew(cram_log)
skew_noncram_log <- manual_skew(noncram_log)
tibble::tibble(
  Group = c("Cram", "Non-Cram"),
  `Raw skewness` = round(c(skew_cram_raw, skew_noncram_raw), 2),
  `Log skewness` = round(c(skew_cram_log, skew_noncram_log), 2)
) %>%
  knitr::kable(align = c("l", "c", "c"), caption = "Skewness before and after log transformation")
```

Skewness is substantially reduced after transformation for both groups. The residual non-normality is likely due to zero-inflation in the raw data, as shown by the clustering of points near zero in Figure 1, which no transformation can remove. Given the large sample size in both groups, the Central Limit Theorem still supports using a t-test.

```{r}
f_test <- var.test(cram_log, noncram_log)
 
tibble::tibble(
  Statistic = round(f_test$statistic, 3),
  p_value = format.pval(f_test$p.value, digits = 3, eps = 0.001)
) %>%
  knitr::kable(align = c("c", "c"), caption = "F test for equality of variances")
```

The F test shows no evidence of unequal variances. We proceed with a Welch two-sample t-test given the large sample size in both groups.

### Test statistic

```{r}
welch_test <- t.test(cram_log, noncram_log, var.equal = FALSE)
welch_test
```

The Welch two-sample t-test statistic is $t = 3.60$ (df = 167.28).

### P-value

The corresponding p-value is $p = 0.0004$. We calculate Cohen's d to quantify the size of this effect.

```{r}
#| label: rq1-effect-size
pooled_sd <- sqrt(((length(cram_log) - 1) * var(cram_log) +
                    (length(noncram_log) - 1) * var(noncram_log)) /
                    (length(cram_log) + length(noncram_log) - 2))
cohend <- (mean(cram_log) - mean(noncram_log)) / pooled_sd
cohend
```

Cohen's d = 0.372, indicating a small-to-medium effect size.

### Conclusion&Limitations
Since $p = 0.0004 < 0.05$, we reject $H_0$. Cram students report significantly higher weekly work hours (log scale) than Non-Cram students, with a small-to-medium effect (Cohen’s $d = 0.372$), suggesting that paid work is one of several factors associated with cramming. Although zero-inflation remained after log transformation, the large sample size supports the t-test approximation. As the data are observational, this association cannot establish that longer work hours cause cramming.

### Hypotheses

$H_0$: multitasking is independent of cramming among DATA2x02 students; $H_1$: the two are not independent.

### Assumptions

Responses from different students are treated as independent.

```{r}
#| label: tbl-rq2-expected
#| tbl-cap: "Expected cell counts under independence."
chi_test$expected %>%
  round(1) %>%
  as.data.frame.matrix() %>%
  tibble::rownames_to_column("Preparation style") %>%
  knitr::kable(
    align = "c",
    col.names = c("Preparation style", "Does not multitask", "Multitasks")
  )
```

The smallest expected count is `r sprintf("%.1f", min_expected)`, so all cells exceed 5 and the approximation is valid; no continuity correction is needed.

### Test statistic

```{r}
#| label: rq2-test
chi_test
```

Under $H_0$, $T=\sum_{i,j}(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}$ follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with `r chi_df` degree of freedom; the observed value is $t_0 = `r sprintf("%.2f", chi_statistic)`$.

### P-value

```{r}
#| label: tbl-rq2-effect-size
#| tbl-cap: "Effect size and confidence interval."
tibble::tibble(
  Measure = c("Cramér's V",
              "Difference in % multitasking (Cram - Non-Cram)"),
  Estimate = c(sprintf("%.3f", cramers_v),
               sprintf("%.1f pp", pct_cram - pct_notcram)),
  `95% CI` = c("-",
               sprintf("[%.1f, %.1f] pp", diff_ci[1], diff_ci[2]))
) %>%
  knitr::kable(align = c("l", "c", "c"))
```

The p-value is `r signif(chi_p, 3)`. Cramér's $V = `r sprintf("%.2f", cramers_v)`$ ($n = `r n_total`$); the 95% CI for the difference in multitasking rates is `r sprintf("%.1f", diff_ci[1])` to `r sprintf("%.1f", diff_ci[2])` percentage points.

### Conclusion

We reject $H_0$: multitasking and cramming are associated, though $V = `r sprintf("%.2f", cramers_v)`$ makes it one correlate of cramming rather than a defining feature.

### Limitations

Both variables are self-reported, so "cram" and "multi-task" may be applied inconsistently, and collapsing `assess` discards information. This observational, voluntary-response survey cannot establish causation — shared workload or procrastination could drive both — and may not generalise.

```{r}
#| label: rq3-permutation-test
#| include: false

rq3_data <- df_clean %>%
  filter(!is.na(work_hrs), !is.na(multitask)) %>%
  mutate(multitask = factor(multitask, levels = c("No", "Yes")))

welch_stat <- function(hours, group) {
  hours_yes <- hours[group == "Yes"]
  hours_no  <- hours[group == "No"]

  (mean(hours_yes) - mean(hours_no)) /
    sqrt(var(hours_yes) / length(hours_yes) +
           var(hours_no) / length(hours_no))
}

mean_yes <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
mean_no  <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
mean_difference <- mean_yes - mean_no
t_observed <- welch_stat(rq3_data$work_hrs, rq3_data$multitask)

set.seed(2002)
B <- 10000

t_permuted <- replicate(
  B,
  welch_stat(
    rq3_data$work_hrs,
    sample(rq3_data$multitask, replace = FALSE)
  )
)

n_yes <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "Yes")
n_no  <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "No")
sd_yes <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
sd_no  <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
pooled_sd <- sqrt(
  ((n_yes - 1) * sd_yes^2 + (n_no - 1) * sd_no^2) /
    (n_yes + n_no - 2)
)
cohens_d <- mean_difference / pooled_sd
```

The two-sided permutation p-value is calculated by counting simulated
statistics at least as extreme as the observed statistic in either direction:

```{r}
#| label: rq3-p-value

extreme_count <- sum(abs(t_permuted) >= abs(t_observed))

# Plus-one correction for a Monte Carlo permutation p-value
p_permutation <- (extreme_count + 1) / (B + 1)

tibble(
  extreme_permutations = extreme_count,
  total_permutations = B,
  permutation_p_value = p_permutation
) %>%
  knitr::kable(
    format = "html",
    digits = c(0, 0, 4),
    col.names = c(
      "Extreme permutations",
      "Total permutations",
      "Permutation p-value"
    ),
    align = c("c", "c", "c"),
    table.attr = 'class="table table-bordered table-striped" style="width: auto; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"'
```

We formally test the difference using a two-sided permutation test with the
Welch $t$-statistic as the resampling statistic.

1. **Hypotheses:** Let $\mu_Y$ and $\mu_N$ be the population mean weekly
   paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask,
   respectively. $H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0$ versus
   $H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0$.

2. **Assumptions:** Responses from different students are treated as
   independent. Under $H_0$, multitasking labels are assumed exchangeable. A
   normal population is not required for the permutation p-value, although the
   mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.

3. **Test statistic:**
   $T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}$. The observed difference
   is `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` hours per week and
   $t_0=`r sprintf("%.3f", t_observed)`$.

4. **Permutation procedure:** With `set.seed(2002)`, the Yes/No labels were
   randomly reassigned $B=10{,}000$ times while employment hours were held
   fixed. The Welch statistic was recalculated each time to form the null
   distribution in @fig-rq3-permutation.

5. **P-value:** For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying
   $|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|$ were counted. The plus-one estimate is
   $p_{\mathrm{perm}}=`r sprintf("%.4f", p_permutation)`$.

6. **Decision:** Since $p<0.05$, we reject $H_0$. There is evidence that mean
   paid-employment hours differ by multitasking status; multitasking students
   worked `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` more hours per week on average
   in this sample. However, Cohen's $d=`r sprintf("%.2f", cohens_d)`$ indicates
   a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are
   self-reported, the 70-hour cleaning threshold is analyst-chosen, and
   confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey of DATA2x02
   students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically generalise
   to other students.

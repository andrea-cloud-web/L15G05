1 Introduction

This report examines how paid employment and study habits relate to students’ university experience, using survey data from DATA2002/2902 students. Three research questions investigate these relationships, with limitations including self-reported data and sampling bias.

1.1 Data Wrangling

All data processing for this report was conducted in R (R Core Team 2026) within the Rmarkdown environment (Xie, Allaire, and Grolemund 2018) for reproducibility. Data wrangling was performed using base R and the tidyverse，Visualisations were produced with ggplot2 (Wickham et al. 2019).

Variables were cleaned and recoded, with missing values handled case-by-case. Import the survey dataset, rename variables for clarity, remove “Other” responses from assess, and remove work_hrs values above 70 hours per week. equivalent to it exceeding a standard full-time work week

manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)
  m <- mean(x)
  (sum((x - m)^3) / n) / (sum((x - m)^2) / n)^(3/2)
}

Normality is still not fully satisfied after transformation, although skewness falls substantially. The remaining non-normality is partly attributable to the large number of zero work-hour responses. Given the large sample sizes, the Central Limit Theorem supports the t-test approximation despite this departure, which is acknowledged as a limitation. Skewness is calculated as: \[\text{skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^3}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2\right)^{3/2}}\]

  1. Conclusion: Since \(p < 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). Cram students report significantly higher weekly work hours (log scale) than Non-Cram students, providing evidence that paid employment is associated with cramming behaviour.

  2. Limitations: Both variables are self-reported, risking recall or social desirability bias. The 70-hour cleaning threshold is analyst-chosen. Normality was not fully achieved even after the log transformation, and reliance on the Central Limit Theorem for validity — rather than genuine normality — means the result is asymptotic rather than exact. As this is observational, cross-sectional data, the result establishes association, not causation — employment may displace study time, or procrastination-prone students may be more likely to seek work. Unmeasured confounders (course load, financial need) cannot be ruled out.

welch_stat <- function(hours, group) {
  hours_yes <- hours[group == "Yes"]
  hours_no  <- hours[group == "No"]

  (mean(hours_yes) - mean(hours_no)) /
    sqrt(var(hours_yes) / length(hours_yes) +
           var(hours_no) / length(hours_no))
}

mean_yes <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
mean_no  <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
mean_difference <- mean_yes - mean_no
t_observed <- welch_stat(rq3_data$work_hrs, rq3_data$multitask)

set.seed(2002)
B <- 10000

t_permuted <- replicate(

  1. Hypotheses: Let \(\mu_Y\) and \(\mu_N\) be the population mean weekly paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask, respectively. \(H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0\) versus \(H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0\).

  2. Assumptions: Student responses are treated as independent, and the multitasking labels are exchangeable under \(H_0\). Normality is unnecessary, although a mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.

  3. Test statistic: \(T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}\). The observed difference is 1.79 hours per week and \(t_0=2.172\).

  4. Permutation procedure: With set.seed(2002), the Yes/No labels were shuffled \(B=10{,}000\) times while employment hours were fixed. The Welch statistic was recalculated to form Figure 3.

  5. P-value: For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying \(|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|\) were counted. The plus-one estimate is \(p_{\mathrm{perm}}=0.0317\).

  6. Decision: Since \(p<0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). There is evidence that mean employment hours differ by multitasking status. multitasking students worked 1.79 more hours per week on average in this sample. However, Cohen’s \(d=0.17\) indicates a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are self-reported and confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey of students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically generalize to others.

3 Conclusion

Overall, the three analyses show consistent links between students’ employment commitments and study behaviours. Students who crammed reported significantly more weekly paid-employment hours, while multitasking was associated with both cramming and higher employment hours. However, the observed effects were modest, suggesting these factors are related but not decisive.

These findings may help the teaching team recognise how students’ external commitments and study habits interact, particularly when considering cramming and lecture multitasking.

However, the survey is observational and relies on self-reported data, limiting causal interpretation. The 70-hour cleaning threshold and categorisation of preparation styles may also affect results. Potential confounding factors, such as workload or other commitments, and voluntary sampling may limit generalisability. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causation.

# Introduction

This report examines how paid employment and study habits relate to students’ university experience, using survey data from DATA2002/2902 students. Three research questions investigate these relationships, with limitations including self-reported data and sampling bias.

## Data Wrangling

All data processing for this report was conducted in R (R Core Team 2026) within the Rmarkdown environment (Xie, Allaire, and Grolemund 2018) for reproducibility. Data wrangling was performed using base R and the tidyverse，Visualisations were produced with ggplot2 (Wickham et al. 2019).

## RQ1: Is there a difference in weekly paid employment hours between students who cram before assessments and those who do not?
Paid employment is a key external time commitment that may shape how students approach assessment preparation (Kember 2004). We compare mean weekly work hours between students who cram before deadlines and those who do not, using a Welch two-sample t-test.

1. **Hypotheses:**
Let $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ be the population mean weekly work hours for Cram and Non-Cram students respectively. $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ vs $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$.

$H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$

2. **Assumptions:**
The Welch t-test assumes approximate normality within each group, assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and QQ plots. Both p-values are below 0.05 and the QQ plots indicate non-normality, so a $\log(x+1)$ transformation is applied to reduce right-skewness.

```{r}
manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)
  m <- mean(x)
  (sum((x - m)^3) / n) / (sum((x - m)^2) / n)^(3/2)
}
```
Normality is still not fully satisfied after transformation, although skewness falls substantially. The remaining non-normality is partly attributable to the large number of zero work-hour responses. Given the large sample sizes, the Central Limit Theorem supports the t-test approximation despite this departure, which is acknowledged as a limitation.
Skewness is calculated as:
$$\text{skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^3}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2\right)^{3/2}}$$

5. **Conclusion:**
Since $p < 0.05$, we reject $H_0$. Cram students report significantly higher weekly work hours (log scale) than Non-Cram students, providing evidence that paid employment is associated with cramming behaviour.  

6. **Limitations:**
Both variables are self-reported, risking recall or social desirability bias. The 70-hour cleaning threshold is analyst-chosen. Normality was not fully achieved even after the log transformation, and reliance on the Central Limit Theorem for validity — rather than genuine normality — means the result is asymptotic rather than exact. As this is observational, cross-sectional data, the result establishes association, not causation — employment may displace study time, or procrastination-prone students may be more likely to seek work. Unmeasured confounders (course load, financial need) cannot be ruled out.

## RQ2: Multitasking and cramming

Our client, a university learning-support team, wants to know whether
multitasking during lectures is associated with cramming for assessments,
to help distinguish a lecture-engagement problem from a broader
time-management one.

```{r}
#| label: rq2-setup
#| message: false
rq2_data <- read_csv("survey_cleaned.csv") %>%
  select(assess, multitask) %>%
  drop_na(assess, multitask) %>%
  mutate(
    assess    = factor(assess, levels = c("Gradually", "Immediately", "Cram")),
    is_cram   = factor(
      if_else(assess == "Cram", "Cram", "Not Cram"),

cram_tab <- table(rq2_data$is_cram, rq2_data$multitask)
chi_test <- chisq.test(cram_tab, correct = FALSE)

chi_statistic <- unname(chi_test$statistic)
chi_df        <- unname(chi_test$parameter)
chi_p         <- chi_test$p.value
min_expected  <- min(chi_test$expected)
n_total       <- sum(cram_tab)
cramers_v     <- sqrt(chi_statistic / n_total)

rq2_props <- rq2_data %>%
  count(is_cram, multitask) %>%

  mutate(prop = n / sum(n)) %>%
  filter(multitask == "Yes")
pct_cram    <- 100 * rq2_props$prop[rq2_props$is_cram == "Cram"]
pct_notcram <- 100 * rq2_props$prop[rq2_props$is_cram == "Not Cram"]
```

- **Hypothesis:** $H_0$: multitasking is independent of cramming vs
  $H_1$: multitasking is not independent of cramming.
- **Assumptions:** Observations are independent (one response per student),
  and expected cell counts must be $\geq 5$ in the $2\times2$ table --
  confirmed below (minimum $\approx$ `r sprintf("%.1f", min_expected)`), so
  the chi-squared approximation is valid.

```{r}
chi_test$expected
```

- **Test statistic:** $T = \sum (O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}$. Under $H_0$,
  $T \sim \chi^2_{`r chi_df`}$.

```{r}
#| label: rq2-test
chi_test
```

- **Observed test statistic:** $t_0 =$ `r sprintf("%.2f", chi_statistic)`
  with `r chi_df` degree of freedom.
- **p-value:** $P(\chi^2_{`r chi_df`} \geq$ `r sprintf("%.2f", chi_statistic)`$)
  =$ `r signif(chi_p, 3)`.
- **Decision:** Reject $H_0$ in favour of $H_1$ ($p < 0.001$). Students who
  cram are considerably more likely to multitask (Cramér's
  $V =$ `r sprintf("%.2f", cramers_v)`, a small-to-moderate effect). As this
  is a self-selected, self-reported survey, "cram" and "multitask" may be
  interpreted inconsistently across respondents, and the association does
  not imply causation -- a third factor such as overall workload could
  plausibly drive both behaviours.

```{r}
#| label: rq3-permutation-test

rq3_data <- df_clean %>%
  filter(!is.na(work_hrs), !is.na(multitask)) %>%
  mutate(multitask = factor(multitask, levels = c("No", "Yes")))

welch_stat <- function(hours, group) {
  hours_yes <- hours[group == "Yes"]
  hours_no  <- hours[group == "No"]

  (mean(hours_yes) - mean(hours_no)) /
    sqrt(var(hours_yes) / length(hours_yes) +
           var(hours_no) / length(hours_no))
}

mean_yes <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
mean_no  <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
mean_difference <- mean_yes - mean_no
t_observed <- welch_stat(rq3_data$work_hrs, rq3_data$multitask)

set.seed(2002)
B <- 10000

t_permuted <- replicate(
  B,
  welch_stat(
    rq3_data$work_hrs,
    sample(rq3_data$multitask, replace = FALSE)
  )
)

n_yes <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "Yes")
n_no  <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "No")
sd_yes <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
sd_no  <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
pooled_sd <- sqrt(
  ((n_yes - 1) * sd_yes^2 + (n_no - 1) * sd_no^2) /
    (n_yes + n_no - 2)
)
cohens_d <- mean_difference / pooled_sd
```

The two-sided permutation p-value is the proportion of simulated statistics at
least as extreme as the observed statistic:

```{r}
#| label: rq3-p-value

extreme_count <- sum(abs(t_permuted) >= abs(t_observed))

# Plus-one correction for a Monte Carlo permutation p-value
p_permutation <- (extreme_count + 1) / (B + 1)

tibble(
  extreme_permutations = extreme_count,
  total_permutations = B,
  permutation_p_value = p_permutation
) %>%
  knitr::kable(

      "Extreme permutations",
      "Total permutations",

    ),

    table.attr = 'class="table table-bordered table-striped" style="width: auto; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"'
```

1. **Hypotheses:** Let $\mu_Y$ and $\mu_N$ be the population mean weekly
   paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask,
   respectively. $H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0$ versus
   $H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0$.

2. **Assumptions:** Student responses are treated as independent, and the
   multitasking labels are exchangeable under $H_0$. Normality is unnecessary,
   although a mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.
   
3. **Test statistic:**
   $T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}$. The observed difference
   is `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` hours per week and
   $t_0=`r sprintf("%.3f", t_observed)`$.

4. **Permutation procedure:** With `set.seed(2002)`, the Yes/No labels were 
   shuffled $B=10{,}000$ times while employment hours were fixed. The Welch 
   statistic was recalculated to form @fig-rq3-permutation.

5. **P-value:** For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying
   $|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|$ were counted. The plus-one estimate is
   $p_{\mathrm{perm}}=`r sprintf("%.4f", p_permutation)`$.

6. **Decision:** Since $p<0.05$, we reject $H_0$. There is evidence that mean
   employment hours differ by multitasking status. multitasking students
   worked `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` more hours per week on average
   in this sample. However, Cohen's $d=`r sprintf("%.2f", cohens_d)`$ indicates
   a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are
   self-reported and confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey 
   of students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically 
   generalize to others.

# Conclusion

Overall, the three analyses show consistent links between students’ employment commitments and study behaviours. Students who crammed reported significantly more weekly paid-employment hours, while multitasking was associated with both cramming and higher employment hours. However, the observed effects were modest, suggesting these factors are related but not decisive.

These findings may help the teaching team recognise how students’ external commitments and study habits interact, particularly when considering cramming and lecture multitasking.

However, the survey is observational and relies on self-reported data, limiting causal interpretation. The 70-hour cleaning threshold and categorisation of preparation styles may also affect results. Potential confounding factors, such as workload or other commitments, and voluntary sampling may limit generalisability. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causation.

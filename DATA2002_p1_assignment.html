1 Introduction

This report examines how paid employment and study habits relate to students’ university experience, using survey data from DATA2002/2902 students. Three research questions investigate these relationships, with limitations including self-reported data and sampling bias.

1.1 Data Wrangling

All data processing for this report was conducted in R (R Core Team 2026) within the Rmarkdown environment (Xie, Allaire, and Grolemund 2018) for reproducibility. Data wrangling was performed using base R and the tidyverse，Visualisations were produced with ggplot2 (Wickham et al. 2019).

Variables were cleaned and recoded, with missing values handled case-by-case. Import the survey dataset, rename variables for clarity, remove “Other” responses from assess, and remove work_hrs values above 70 hours per week. equivalent to it exceeding a standard full-time work week

manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)
  m <- mean(x)
  (sum((x - m)^3) / n) / (sum((x - m)^2) / n)^(3/2)
}

Normality is still not fully satisfied after transformation, although skewness falls substantially. The remaining non-normality is partly attributable to the large number of zero work-hour responses. Given the large sample sizes, the Central Limit Theorem supports the t-test approximation despite this departure, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Skewness is calculated as: \[\text{skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^3}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2\right)^{3/2}}\]

Our client, a university learning-support team, wants to distinguish a lecture-engagement problem from a broader time-management one. Multitasking in lectures is usually read as disengagement, making it a plausible correlate of poor preparation habits, which we test with a chi-squared test of independence.

welch_stat <- function(hours, group) {
  hours_yes <- hours[group == "Yes"]
  hours_no  <- hours[group == "No"]

  (mean(hours_yes) - mean(hours_no)) /
    sqrt(var(hours_yes) / length(hours_yes) +
           var(hours_no) / length(hours_no))
}

mean_yes <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
mean_no  <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
mean_difference <- mean_yes - mean_no
t_observed <- welch_stat(rq3_data$work_hrs, rq3_data$multitask)

set.seed(2002)

  1. Hypotheses: Let \(\mu_Y\) and \(\mu_N\) be the population mean weekly paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask, respectively. \(H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0\) versus \(H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0\).

  2. Assumptions: Student responses are treated as independent, and the multitasking labels are exchangeable under \(H_0\). Normality is unnecessary, although a mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.

  3. Test statistic: \(T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}\). The observed difference is 1.79 hours per week and \(t_0=2.172\).

  4. Permutation procedure: With set.seed(2002), the Yes/No labels were shuffled \(B=10{,}000\) times while employment hours were fixed. The Welch statistic was recalculated to form Figure 3.

  5. P-value: For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying \(|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|\) were counted. The plus-one estimate is \(p_{\mathrm{perm}}=0.0317\).

  6. Decision: Since \(p<0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). There is evidence that mean employment hours differ by multitasking status. multitasking students worked 1.79 more hours per week on average in this sample. However, Cohen’s \(d=0.17\) indicates a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are self-reported and confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey of students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically generalize to others.

3 Conclusion

Overall, the three analyses show consistent links between students’ employment commitments and study behaviours. Students who crammed reported significantly more weekly paid-employment hours, while multitasking was associated with both cramming and higher employment hours. However, the observed effects were modest, suggesting these factors are related but not decisive.

These findings may help the teaching team recognise how students’ external commitments and study habits interact, particularly when considering cramming and lecture multitasking.

However, the survey is observational and relies on self-reported data, limiting causal interpretation. The 70-hour cleaning threshold and categorisation of preparation styles may also affect results. Potential confounding factors, such as workload or other commitments, and voluntary sampling may limit generalisability. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causation.

manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)

welch_stat <- function(hours, group) {
  hours_yes <- hours[group == "Yes"]
  hours_no  <- hours[group == "No"]

  (mean(hours_yes) - mean(hours_no)) /
    sqrt(var(hours_yes) / length(hours_yes) +
           var(hours_no) / length(hours_no))
}

mean_yes <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
mean_no  <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
mean_difference <- mean_yes - mean_no
t_observed <- welch_stat(rq3_data$work_hrs, rq3_data$multitask)

set.seed(2002)
B <- 10000

t_permuted <- replicate(
  B,
  welch_stat(
    rq3_data$work_hrs,
    sample(rq3_data$multitask, replace = FALSE)
  )
)

n_yes <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "Yes")
n_no  <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "No")
sd_yes <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
sd_no  <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
pooled_sd <- sqrt(
  ((n_yes - 1) * sd_yes^2 + (n_no - 1) * sd_no^2) /
    (n_yes + n_no - 2)
)
cohens_d <- mean_difference / pooled_sd

1. **Hypotheses:** Let $\mu_Y$ and $\mu_N$ be the population mean weekly

   although a mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.
   
3. **Test statistic:**
   $T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}$. The observed difference
   is `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` hours per week and
   $t_0=`r sprintf("%.3f", t_observed)`$.

4. **Permutation procedure:** With `set.seed(2002)`, the Yes/No labels were 
   shuffled $B=10{,}000$ times while employment hours were fixed. The Welch 
   statistic was recalculated to form @fig-rq3-permutation.

5. **P-value:** For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying
   $|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|$ were counted. The plus-one estimate is
   $p_{\mathrm{perm}}=`r sprintf("%.4f", p_permutation)`$.

6. **Decision:** Since $p<0.05$, we reject $H_0$. There is evidence that mean
   employment hours differ by multitasking status. multitasking students
   worked `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` more hours per week on average
   in this sample. However, Cohen's $d=`r sprintf("%.2f", cohens_d)`$ indicates
   a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are
   self-reported and confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey 
   of students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically 
   generalize to others.

# Conclusion

Overall, the three analyses show consistent links between students’ employment commitments and study behaviours. Students who crammed reported significantly more weekly paid-employment hours, while multitasking was associated with both cramming and higher employment hours. However, the observed effects were modest, suggesting these factors are related but not decisive.

These findings may help the teaching team recognise how students’ external commitments and study habits interact, particularly when considering cramming and lecture multitasking.

However, the survey is observational and relies on self-reported data, limiting causal interpretation. The 70-hour cleaning threshold and categorisation of preparation styles may also affect results. Potential confounding factors, such as workload or other commitments, and voluntary sampling may limit generalisability. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causation.

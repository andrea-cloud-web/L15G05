1 Introduction

This report examines how paid employment and study habits relate to students’ university experience, using survey data from DATA2002/2902 students. Three research questions investigate these relationships, with limitations including self-reported data and sampling bias.

1.1 Data Wrangling

All data processing for this report was conducted in R (R Core Team 2026) within the Rmarkdown environment (Xie, Allaire, and Grolemund 2018) for reproducibility. Data wrangling was performed using base R and the tidyverse，Visualisations were produced with ggplot2 (Wickham et al. 2019).

Variables were cleaned and recoded, with missing values handled case-by-case. Import the survey dataset, rename variables for clarity, remove “Other” responses from assess, and remove work_hrs values above 70 hours per week. equivalent to it exceeding a standard full-time work week

2.1 RQ1: Work hours and cramming

Research Question: Is there a difference in weekly paid employment hours between students who cram before assessments and those who do not?

2.1.2 Hypotheses

Let \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) be the population mean weekly work hours for Cram and Non-Cram students respectively. \(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\) vs \(H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2\).

\(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\)
\(H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2\)

2.1.3 Assumptions

The Welch t-test assumes approximate normality within each group. Shapiro-Wilk tests on raw work hours are highly significant for both groups (Table 2), indicating strong right-skew, so we apply a log(x+1) transformation. Skewness falls substantially after transformation (Table 4: Cram 1.12→-0.11, Non-Cram 1.98→0.60), though normality is not fully achieved, likely due to zero-inflation — many students report zero weekly work hours (Figure 1), a pattern no monotonic transform removes. Given the large sample sizes (\(n_{cram}=117\), \(n_{non\text{-}cram}=544\)), the Central Limit Theorem supports t-test validity regardless. An F test shows no evidence of unequal variances (\(p=0.749\)), though we retain the Welch form for robustness.

manual_skew <- function(x) {
  n <- length(x)
  m <- mean(x)
  (sum((x - m)^3) / n) / (sum((x - m)^2) / n)^(3/2)
}

2.1.6 Conclusion&Limitations

Since \(p = 0.0004 < 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). Cram students report significantly higher weekly work hours (log scale) than Non-Cram students, with a small-to-medium effect (Cohen’s \(d = 0.372\)), suggesting that paid work is one of several factors associated with cramming. Although zero-inflation remained after log transformation, the large sample size supports the t-test approximation. As the data are observational, this association cannot establish that longer work hours cause cramming.

2.1.7 Conclusion

Since \(p < 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). Cram students report significantly higher weekly work hours (log scale) than Non-Cram students, providing evidence that paid employment is associated with cramming behaviour.

2.1.8 Limitations

Limitations: Both variables are self-reported, risking recall or social desirability bias. The 70-hour cleaning threshold is analyst-chosen. Normality was not fully achieved even after the log transformation, and reliance on the Central Limit Theorem for validity — rather than genuine normality — means the result is asymptotic rather than exact. As this is observational, cross-sectional data, the result establishes association, not causation — employment may displace study time, or procrastination-prone students may be more likely to seek work. Unmeasured confounders (course load, financial need) cannot be ruled out.

2.2 RQ2: Multitasking and cramming

Research Question: Is multitasking during lectures associated with students’ assessment preparation style, specifically whether they tend to cram before deadlines?

Multitasking during lectures (e.g. using phones or other devices) is often seen as a sign of disengagement or poor time management. This research question examines whether multitasking behaviour is associated with cramming, since students who habitually delay their revision may be more likely to disengage during class as deadlines approach. Understanding this link could help the teaching team judge whether lecture engagement issues are connected to broader time-management or study-habit problems, rather than treating them as separate concerns. A chi-squared test of independence is used to test whether multitasking behaviour and cramming behaviour are associated.

2.2.2 Hypotheses

Let multitasking status and cramming status be the two categorical variables summarised in Table 1.

\(H_0\): multitasking behaviour is independent of cramming behaviour. \(H_1\): multitasking behaviour is not independent of cramming behaviour.

2.2.3 Assumptions

The chi-squared test of independence assumes that observations are independent, with one row per student, which is reasonable here as no student appears twice in the survey. It also requires expected cell counts of at least 5 in every cell of the contingency table.

Code 
chi_test$expected
          
                  No       Yes
  Not Cram 239.49168 304.50832
  Cram      51.50832  65.49168

The smallest expected count is 51.5, so all expected counts are comfortably above 5 and the chi-squared approximation is valid. Neither Fisher’s exact test nor a Yates continuity correction is required.

2.2.4 Test statistic

Code 
chi_test

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  cram_tab
X-squared = 21.351, df = 1, p-value = 3.825e-06

The test statistic is \(T=\sum_{i,j}(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}\), which under \(H_0\) follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \((r-1)(c-1) = 1\) degree of freedom. The observed value is \(t_0 = 21.35\).

2.2.5 P-value

The corresponding p-value is \(P(\chi^2_{1} \geq 21.35) = 3.82\times 10^{-6}\). We calculate Cramér’s V to quantify the size of this effect.

Code 
cramers_v
[1] 0.1797235

Cramér’s \(V = 0.18\) based on \(n = 661\) complete responses, indicating a small-to-moderate association.

2.2.6 Conclusion

Since \(p < 0.001\), we reject \(H_0\). There is strong evidence of an association between multitasking during lectures and cramming: students who cram were substantially more likely to multitask (75.2%) than students who did not cram (51.8%) in this sample. Cramér’s \(V = 0.18\) indicates that the strength of this association is nonetheless modest, so multitasking should be read as one of several correlates of cramming rather than a defining feature of it.

2.2.7 Limitations

Both variables are self-reported and self-interpreted, so respondents may apply the labels “cram” and “multi-task” inconsistently. Collapsing assess from three levels to two also discards some information about the distinction between preparing gradually and preparing immediately. As this is an observational, voluntary-response survey of DATA2x02 students, the association cannot establish causation — a third factor such as overall workload or procrastination tendency could plausibly drive both behaviours — and it may not generalise beyond this cohort.

We formally test the difference using a two-sided permutation test with the Welch \(t\)-statistic as the resampling statistic.

  1. Hypotheses: Let \(\mu_Y\) and \(\mu_N\) be the population mean weekly paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask, respectively. \(H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0\) versus \(H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0\).

  2. Assumptions: Responses from different students are treated as independent. Under \(H_0\), multitasking labels are assumed exchangeable. A normal population is not required for the permutation p-value, although the mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.

  3. Test statistic: \(T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}\). The observed difference is 1.79 hours per week and \(t_0=2.172\).

  4. Permutation procedure: With set.seed(2002), the Yes/No labels were randomly reassigned \(B=10{,}000\) times while employment hours were held fixed. The Welch statistic was recalculated each time to form the null distribution in Figure 4.

  5. P-value: For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying \(|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|\) were counted. The plus-one estimate is \(p_{\mathrm{perm}}=0.0317\).

  6. Decision: Since \(p<0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). However, Cohen’s \(d=0.17\) indicates a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. ### Assumptions

The Welch t-test assumes approximate normality within each group. Shapiro-Wilk tests on raw work hours are highly significant for both groups (Table 2), indicating strong right-skew, so we apply a log(x+1) transformation. ## RQ2: Multitasking and cramming

**Research Question:** Is multitasking during lectures associated with students' assessment preparation style, specifically whether they tend to cram before deadlines?

Multitasking during lectures (e.g. using phones or other devices) is often seen as a sign of disengagement or poor time management. This research question examines whether multitasking behaviour is associated with cramming, since students who habitually delay their revision may be more likely to disengage during class as deadlines approach. Understanding this link could help the teaching team judge whether lecture engagement issues are connected to broader time-management or study-habit problems, rather than treating them as separate concerns. ### Hypotheses

Let multitasking status and cramming status be the two categorical variables summarised in @tbl-rq2-table.

$H_0$: multitasking behaviour is independent of cramming behaviour. $H_1$: multitasking behaviour is not independent of cramming behaviour.

### Assumptions

The chi-squared test of independence assumes that observations are independent, with one row per student, which is reasonable here as no student appears twice in the survey. It also requires expected cell counts of at least 5 in every cell of the contingency table.

```{r}
#| label: rq2-assumptions
chi_test$expected
```

The smallest expected count is `r sprintf("%.1f", min_expected)`, so all expected counts are comfortably above 5 and the chi-squared approximation is valid. Neither Fisher's exact test nor a Yates continuity correction is required.

### Test statistic

```{r}
#| label: rq2-test
chi_test
```

The test statistic is $T=\sum_{i,j}(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}$, which under $H_0$ follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $(r-1)(c-1) = `r chi_df`$ degree of freedom. The observed value is $t_0 = `r sprintf("%.2f", chi_statistic)`$.

### P-value

The corresponding p-value is $P(\chi^2_{`r chi_df`} \geq `r sprintf("%.2f", chi_statistic)`) = `r signif(chi_p, 3)`$. We calculate Cramér's V to quantify the size of this effect.

```{r}
#| label: rq2-effect-size
cramers_v
```

Cramér's $V = `r sprintf("%.2f", cramers_v)`$ based on $n = `r n_total`$ complete responses, indicating a small-to-moderate association.

### Conclusion

Since $p < 0.001$, we reject $H_0$. There is strong evidence of an association between multitasking during lectures and cramming: students who cram were substantially more likely to multitask (`r sprintf("%.1f", pct_cram)`%) than students who did not cram (`r sprintf("%.1f", pct_notcram)`%) in this sample. Cramér's $V = `r sprintf("%.2f", cramers_v)`$ indicates that the strength of this association is nonetheless modest, so multitasking should be read as one of several correlates of cramming rather than a defining feature of it.

### Limitations

Both variables are self-reported and self-interpreted, so respondents may apply the labels "cram" and "multi-task" inconsistently. Collapsing `assess` from three levels to two also discards some information about the distinction between preparing gradually and preparing immediately. As this is an observational, voluntary-response survey of DATA2x02 students, the association cannot establish causation — a third factor such as overall workload or procrastination tendency could plausibly drive both behaviours — and it may not generalise beyond this cohort.

```{r}
#| label: rq3-permutation-test
#| include: false

rq3_data <- df_clean %>%
  filter(!is.na(work_hrs), !is.na(multitask)) %>%
  mutate(multitask = factor(multitask, levels = c("No", "Yes")))

welch_stat <- function(hours, group) {
  hours_yes <- hours[group == "Yes"]
  hours_no  <- hours[group == "No"]

  (mean(hours_yes) - mean(hours_no)) /
    sqrt(var(hours_yes) / length(hours_yes) +
           var(hours_no) / length(hours_no))
}

mean_yes <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
mean_no  <- mean(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
mean_difference <- mean_yes - mean_no
t_observed <- welch_stat(rq3_data$work_hrs, rq3_data$multitask)

set.seed(2002)
B <- 10000

t_permuted <- replicate(
  B,
  welch_stat(
    rq3_data$work_hrs,
    sample(rq3_data$multitask, replace = FALSE)
  )
)

n_yes <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "Yes")
n_no  <- sum(rq3_data$multitask == "No")
sd_yes <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "Yes"])
sd_no  <- sd(rq3_data$work_hrs[rq3_data$multitask == "No"])
pooled_sd <- sqrt(
  ((n_yes - 1) * sd_yes^2 + (n_no - 1) * sd_no^2) /
    (n_yes + n_no - 2)
)
cohens_d <- mean_difference / pooled_sd
```

The two-sided permutation p-value is calculated by counting simulated
statistics at least as extreme as the observed statistic in either direction:

```{r}
#| label: rq3-p-value

extreme_count <- sum(abs(t_permuted) >= abs(t_observed))

# Plus-one correction for a Monte Carlo permutation p-value
p_permutation <- (extreme_count + 1) / (B + 1)

tibble(
  extreme_permutations = extreme_count,
  total_permutations = B,
  permutation_p_value = p_permutation
) %>%
  knitr::kable(
    format = "html",
    digits = c(0, 0, 4),
    col.names = c(
      "Extreme permutations",
      "Total permutations",
      "Permutation p-value"
    ),
    align = c("c", "c", "c"),
    table.attr = 'class="table table-bordered table-striped" style="width: auto; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"'
  )
```

We formally test the difference using a two-sided permutation test with the
Welch $t$-statistic as the resampling statistic.

1. **Hypotheses:** Let $\mu_Y$ and $\mu_N$ be the population mean weekly
   paid-employment hours for students who do and do not multitask,
   respectively. $H_0:\mu_Y-\mu_N=0$ versus
   $H_A:\mu_Y-\mu_N\neq0$.

2. **Assumptions:** Responses from different students are treated as
   independent. Under $H_0$, multitasking labels are assumed exchangeable. A
   normal population is not required for the permutation p-value, although the
   mean-based statistic remains sensitive to large values.

3. **Test statistic:**
   $T=(\bar X_Y-\bar X_N)/\sqrt{S_Y^2/n_Y+S_N^2/n_N}$. The observed difference
   is `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` hours per week and
   $t_0=`r sprintf("%.3f", t_observed)`$.

4. **Permutation procedure:** With `set.seed(2002)`, the Yes/No labels were
   randomly reassigned $B=10{,}000$ times while employment hours were held
   fixed. The Welch statistic was recalculated each time to form the null
   distribution in @fig-rq3-permutation.

5. **P-value:** For the two-sided test, permutations satisfying
   $|T_b^*|\geq|t_0|$ were counted. The plus-one estimate is
   $p_{\mathrm{perm}}=`r sprintf("%.4f", p_permutation)`$.

6. **Decision:** Since $p<0.05$, we reject $H_0$. There is evidence that mean
   paid-employment hours differ by multitasking status; multitasking students
   worked `r sprintf("%.2f", mean_difference)` more hours per week on average
   in this sample. However, Cohen's $d=`r sprintf("%.2f", cohens_d)`$ indicates
   a small effect, so its practical importance is limited. Both variables are
   self-reported, the 70-hour cleaning threshold is analyst-chosen, and
   confounding may remain. As this is an observational survey of DATA2x02
   students, the result cannot establish causation or automatically generalise
   to other students.

# Conclusion

Overall, the three analyses show that greater employment commitments were associated with more cramming and lecture multitasking, although the effects were modest.

These findings suggest that students’ employment and study habits may provide useful indicators of academic engagement and help teaching staff understand students’ study behaviours.

However, as the survey is observational and self-reported, the findings show associations rather than causation, with sampling bias, self-reporting, and confounding factors limiting generalisability.
